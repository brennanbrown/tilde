SDF, the Super Dimension Fortress, is a public access UNIX system that has been running continuously since 1987. Self-described as "a community platform for inspiring, facilitating and implementing new ideas". It's a multi-user NetBSD machine, funded solely by its members as a 501(c)(7) nonprofit, where tens of thousands of people hold accounts. The name comes from the anime Super Dimension Fortress Macross (known in the West as part of Robotech), and the system began as a BBS for Japanese anime fans. Its BBS-era roots were documented in the 2004 film BBS: The Documentary, which includes a recorded interview with Stephen Jones, SDF's longtime sysadmin.

History

  • June 16, 1987: Ted Uhlemann ("iczer") plugs an Apple IIe's 300-baud modem into a phone line his mother had just given him for his birthday, running "Magic City Micro-BBS" (written in Applesoft BASIC). He names it SDF-1 after his favorite anime, hoping to attract fans of "anime, industrial music and the Church of the SubGenius," per a 30th-anniversary retrospective by Stephen Jones. The original hardware: one 1200bps modem and two floppy drives holding 280 kilobytes each.
  • 1989: Stephen Jones (smj) finds a 5-megabyte mass storage unit for the Apple; Ted and Stephen start running SDF as a Citadel BBS, drawn to its filesystem-like message structure.
  • Feb 20, 1990: Ted, Stephen, and Daniel Finster had been dialing into The UNIX Connection ("killer.dallas.tx.us"), a public-access UNIX box run by AT&T employee Charlie Boykin. When federal agents shut it down during Operation Sundevil (the same 1990 crackdown chronicled in Bruce Sterling's The Hacker Crackdown), the three decided to build a replacement.
  • 1990: They stand up a real UNIX System V BBS, first on an i386 (after rejecting the UNIX clone Coherent as "a CROCK," in the FAQ's words), then on an AT&T 3B2/400 and 500, joining the lonestar.org UUCP network for mail and news.
  • 1992: Ted, Daniel, and three SDF users leave to found Texas Metronet, Dallas's first commercial ISP. Stephen Jones stays and keeps running SDF, a role he still holds decades later.
  • 1993–96: The system moves to AT&T 3B2 minicomputers networked over STARLAN, expands to nine phone lines, and briefly spins up a side project called ANONBBS, an anonymous message board on a Toshiba laptop that Ted and Stephen "hashed out over tacos and nachos at 3am in a Taco Cabana."
  • 1997: With 15,000 users, SDF migrates to Linux. Jones calls this "the dark age" as the system starts coming under attack like never before.
  • 1999: The freeshell.org domain is registered as a marketing alias after members vote to create the ARPA membership tier.
  • Aug 9–10, 2001: The Linux/x86 era ends ("BYE BYE LEENOX! BYE BYE x86!" reads the ASCII-art commit message) and SDF moves to NetBSD on DEC Alpha hardware, relocating from Lewisville, Texas to Seattle, Washington.
  • Oct 1, 2001: SDF Public Access UNIX System, Inc. is formed as a Delaware not-for-profit and later granted 501(c)(7) membership-club status by the IRS.
  • Jan 30, 2003: SDF's co-location provider, NWLink, abruptly terminates the hosting contract with no written notice, citing a DDoS attack aimed at SDF. The FAQ history calls the shutdown immediate: "approximately 10:30am." SiteSpecific.NET donates emergency free hosting, and SDF is back online by February 4th. Members are still invited to read the full account and file a complaint with the Better Business Bureau if they wish.
  • March 2003: SDF relocates back to Dallas onto new, member-funded machines, on two independent circuits, specifically so no single ISP can pull the plug again.
  • 2016: 47,572 users worldwide.
  • 2018: SDF becomes the largest NetBSD installation in the world and an official NetBSD development site.

A university researcher's 2019 survey of public-access UNIX systems, History, Evolution and Future of Public Access UNIX, hosted on SDF itself, describes the modern system as "a fully articulated social community," noting SDF supports special interest groups for ham radio (SDFARC), Python, Gopher enthusiasts, and Minecraft players alongside "digital artists, computer historians, web developers, gamers, and of course many, many programmers." SDF's own pictorial tour documents the hardware year by year back to 1985, including a stint at a telephone museum and the machines running today.

Today the network includes NetBSD servers on DEC Alpha and AMD Opteron hardware, plus live retrocomputing environments: a TOPS-20 system (twenex.org, running Panda Distribution TOPS-20 on two XKL TOAD-2s), a Symbolics Genera machine, and an ITS system. Free accounts are available on all of them.

Culture

SDF's culture is closer to a university shell account than to a modern community site. The institutions are old and still alive:

  • bboard: the command-line message board of SDF business, tech help, and thirty years of in-jokes
  • com: SDF's own chat program, a DEC TOPS-20 commode system that Jones ported to UNIX as a KornShell script. It predates IRC on the system, and is still where the regulars are
  • maint: account maintenance; run to see your membership level, set passwords, enable memberships
  • wiki.sdf.org: member-written documentation going back decades
  • happening: an anonymous blogging tool built for the community, and oneliner, a running one-line-at-a-time log members post to

In a 2006 bsdtalk interview, one longtime member summed up what makes the place stick around. "SDF is more than a shell account, it's a community of people who communicate with one another via the various methods that SDF provides... What makes SDF special is that it is run like a computing environment in the early days of the Internet. It's not corporate at all."

Why?

SDF is one of the last places offering free dial-up access. Wikipedia calls SDF "one of very few organizations in the world still actively promoting the gopher protocol," and classrooms also get free UNIX accounts for teaching. SDF is a computer owned by its users, where the tools are close to forty years of UNIX rather than a feed. The community still adds services (Matrix, Gitea, Mastodon, PeerTube, Lemmy, etc.) alongside what's been running since 1989, and it has survived hardware failures, a hostile termination by its own ISP, and multiple cross-country moves without ever losing its user base or going commercial. Jones, still running the system decades in, put it simply in his 30th-anniversary letter: "Some people today may come here and see us as outdated and 'retro.' But if you get involved, you'll see it is quite alive with new ideas."

Sign Up

Free shell accounts are created on demand:

  • ssh new@sdf.org (any OS)
  • ssh.sdf.org, HTML5 SSH client, no install needed
  • Stuck? Live help on irc.sdf.org, channel #helpdesk

Then say hello on com, post on bboard, and read the FAQ, covering everything from GAMES to VHOST.
